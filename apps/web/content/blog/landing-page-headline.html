Visitors decide whether a page is for them almost immediately. They are not reading; they are scanning for one thing: "is this for someone like me?" The headline is the first and often the only place they look for the answer.

Most founder headlines fail in one of two ways. They are vague ("The smarter way to grow") or they are clever in a way only the founder understands. This post gives you a short set of tips and a test to fix both.

What is the job of a landing page headline?

A landing page headline tells the right visitor, in plain words, what you help them do. That is the whole job. It does not need to explain every feature, sound impressive or win an award; it needs to make the right person think "yes, that's my problem."

If you want a clearer picture of who "the right person" is, start with your ideal customer profile. A headline written for everyone lands with no one.

What makes a headline clear?

A clear headline names a specific person or situation, states an outcome, and uses words that person would say out loud. The checklist below covers the pieces.

  • Name who it is for. "For solo founders" or "for two-person support teams" filters in the right visitors and tells the wrong ones they can leave.
  • State the outcome, not the mechanism. People buy the thing that changes for them, not the technology under it.
  • Use their words. Copy phrases from emails, calls and community posts. If they say "chasing invoices," do not write "accounts receivable optimization."
  • Be concrete. A specific noun beats an abstract one every time.
  • Skip the filler. "Revolutionary," "next-generation" and "all-in-one" tell the reader nothing they can check.

How do you write a headline from scratch?

Fill in one plain sentence first, then trim it until it reads like something a person would say. Do not open a blank page and wait for inspiration.

  1. Write the sentence: "We help [specific person] [do something they want] without [the thing they hate]."
  2. Collect three real phrases your customers use for the problem, and swap them in for any jargon.
  3. Write five versions. Make each one differ in angle: outcome-first, pain-first, audience-first, before/after, and a plain description.
  4. Cut every word you could remove without losing meaning.
  5. Move the detail down. The headline carries the promise; the subheadline carries what the product is and how it works.

How do you test a headline before you publish?

Use the five-second test: show the page to someone in your target group, take it away, and ask what it is and who it is for. If they cannot tell you, the headline is not done.

You do not need traffic for this. Three or four people from the community where your customers hang out is enough to catch the worst problems. Pay attention to the words they use when they describe your product back to you; those words often beat your draft.

Once the page is live, change one thing at a time and give each version enough visitors before you judge it. Swapping the headline, button and image together tells you nothing about which change mattered.

What mistakes should you avoid?

The common ones are trying to say everything, hiding the audience, and using humor before anyone knows who you are. A joke only works if the visitor already understands what you sell.

  • Putting the company name or tagline where the promise should be.
  • Writing for investors or competitors instead of the buyer.
  • Listing features in the headline instead of one clear result.
  • Changing the headline every few days, so nothing is ever tested.

The same clarity applies beyond the page. A short video or post that makes the same promise in the same words reinforces it, as covered in short-form video for founders.

Summary

Write for one person, name the outcome they want, use their words, and test with five seconds and a stranger. A headline that makes the right visitor feel understood does more than a clever one that makes everyone admire you.
